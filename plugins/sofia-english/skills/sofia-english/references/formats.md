# Recording formats

A format describes how a video is recorded and presented. A structure describes information order; editorial principles describe its effect. Choose a feasible format before writing the narrative. These are practical options, not a claim that every format is officially validated or guarantees results.

| Format | What it needs | Writing rule | Suitable structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behind the scenes | Show the real process behind an outcome. | Open with the outcome or a concrete obstacle; show actual steps and close the gap. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Question box | Answer a real or clearly labeled illustrative audience question. | Quote the question accurately, answer it directly and demonstrate the reason. | Wrong way, right way |
| Cinematic | Tell a story through composed shots, sound and action. | Write actions that can be filmed; let images carry information rather than narrating everything. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Comparison | Compare two options using consistent criteria. | State the criterion, show both sides and explain the tradeoff with real evidence. | Analysis |
| Dialogue | The creator plays a conversation between characters. | Each voice has a clear intention; label fictional dramatization rather than presenting it as a real exchange. | Wrong way, right way |
| Fast-paced | Use quick visual changes and concise spoken beats. | Each cut adds information; keep text readable and do not equate speed with retention. | Attention, interest, desire, action |
| Social experiment | Observe a real interaction or behavior. | Use an actual setup, consent where needed and observed outcomes; never fabricate participants or reactions. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Voice-over | Narration over footage, images or screen recordings. | Match each claim to relevant visual evidence and identify footage still needed. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Direct to camera | A creator talks directly to the viewer. | Lead with a concrete idea and make the argument work without missing visual assets. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Visual storytelling | Graphics and text help carry a story. | Specify what each graphic communicates and keep spoken and visual information complementary. | Them, me, you, future |
| Split screen | A reference and the creator appear side by side. | Select a specific reference moment, preserve its context and distinguish observation from interpretation. | Analysis |
| Green screen | A reference image or clip appears behind the presenter. | Use a legible reference, point to the relevant evidence and avoid covering essential details. | Analysis |
| Mockumentary | A staged documentary-style scene with asides to camera. | Use a coherent character intention and purposeful asides; label staging when factual interpretation could be misleading. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Situational conflict | Start inside an urgent or recognizable situation. | Make the obstacle visible and resolve the same conflict; do not present staged danger as real. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Everyday conversation | A casual conversation in an ordinary setting. | Keep the situation specific and let a meaningful turn emerge naturally. | Them, me, you, future |
| Low production | Deliberately simple filming with minimal polish. | Prioritize audible speech, a readable image and a concrete message; roughness alone is not authenticity. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Vlog | Document a real experience across moments and settings. | Use observed events and causal transitions, not an invented day or reconstructed outcome passed off as real. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Analysis | Interpret a defined reference or case. | State a thesis, support distinct reasons with observed evidence and give a transferable takeaway. | Analysis |
| Off-camera conversation | Speak informally toward someone beside the lens. | Keep one clear listener and a natural rhythm without pretending an unscripted interview occurred. | Them, me, you, future |
| Analogy | Use something concrete to explain an abstract idea. | State where the comparison holds and avoid using it as factual proof beyond those limits. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Visual opening, simple body | A strong visual opening leads into simple delivery. | The image must be relevant and its promise must be fulfilled by the body. | Attention, interest, desire, action |
| Narrator and character | A character reacts to an off-screen narrator. | Write two distinct roles, purposeful interruption and a clear resolution. | Opening, story, conclusion |
| Combined format | Combine two recognizable recording approaches. | Use each for a specific job and preserve continuity when switching. | Choose by the central idea |

For visual proof, consider comparison, green screen or visual storytelling. For lived experience, consider vlog, voice-over or behind the scenes. For an argument, consider direct to camera, analysis or a question box. For a reference reaction, consider split screen. If the ideal format exceeds the creator’s resources, suggest the closest feasible alternative and explain the tradeoff.
