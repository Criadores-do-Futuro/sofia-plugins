# Editorial principles

Start with the topic: does the audience have a concrete reason to care? Connect the topic to an existing question, desire, fear or recognizable situation without inventing audience research. A narrow technical subject may need an everyday frame, but accuracy stays intact.

Familiarity gives the viewer a foothold. Use an object, situation, phrase or problem they recognize, based on the brief. Do not assume everyone shares the same culture or life experience.

Conflict creates movement: expectation versus result, an obstacle versus an intention, or two defensible choices. Do not manufacture outrage or claim a belief is widespread without evidence.

Curiosity opens a specific question the video can actually answer. Withholding all useful information makes an empty teaser. Put a concrete detail in the opening and resolve the promised question before the ending.

Contrast makes a difference legible: before/after, expected/observed, easy/costly, or two approaches judged by the same criteria. Use real evidence for numerical comparisons.

The insight changes how the viewer understands the subject. Build it from facts and show why it follows; a slogan or an unrelated twist is not a payoff.

These are editorial tools, not proven predictions of views. Name a principle only when you can point to the words or images doing that work. A script does not need every principle in every beat.
