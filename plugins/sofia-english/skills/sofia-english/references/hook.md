# How to make the hook

The hook is the first second of the video: the spoken line, the on-screen text and what appears. This piece is the base of every hook. The other hook pieces ([hooks that work](knowledge/hooks-that-work.md), [breaking the bubble](breaking-the-bubble.md) and the format of the video brief) say which type of opening to use; this one says how to build the opening so the viewer stops scrolling. What comes right after the hook, so the viewer stays, is in [the hold zone](hold-zone.md).

## 1. The only goal: break the autopilot

Someone scrolling the feed is on autopilot, almost in a trance: one video after another, deciding nothing. The hook breaks that trance with something the viewer didn't expect: an image, a sentence or a contrast outside the feed's pattern. Their attention turns to what changed before they decide, as an automatic reaction to the unexpected. In that instant the viewer freezes: the thumb comes off the screen and they decide whether to pay attention.

The hook doesn't tell the whole story. It convinces the viewer to want to hear the next sentence.

## 2. The two questions of the first 2 seconds

In the instant they freeze, the scroller answers two questions, without noticing, before deciding to stay:

1. **What is this video about?** The subject shows up right away.
2. **What do I get if I keep watching?** A solution, a practical benefit or something curious.

The hook raises both and answers both. A hook that leaves one of them unanswered loses the viewer, even with a beautiful sentence.

## 3. The eye arrives before the ear

The eyes read the image and the on-screen text faster than the ear understands speech. So the hook happens in three beats:

1. the viewer sees the image and reads the on-screen text;
2. hears the spoken line;
3. looks at the screen again, to check that the line matches what they saw.

Image, on-screen text and spoken line tell the same thing. If the image shows one thing, the text says another and the line is about a third, the viewer gets confused and swipes.

## 4. The four mistakes that kill a hook

- **HK9.** **Taking too long to say the subject.** A greeting ("hey guys", "what's up everyone"), an introduction or a detour like "today I saw the most unbelievable thing in the world", without saying the subject. The audience leaves in the first 2 seconds. Cut whatever stalls and say the exact subject in the first or second second.
- **HK10.** **Confusing.** A long sentence, in the passive voice, cut in the middle or with two meanings. If the viewer has to stop to decode it, they swipe. Active voice, everyday words and the fewest words possible.
- **HK11.** **Seeming like it's not for them.** The viewer understands the subject but doesn't see themselves in it. It happens a lot when the creator talks about themselves: "I have this problem" creates distance. Talk to the viewer ("If you deal with this problem", "your business") and anchor the sentence to a pain they know or a practical result they want.
- **HK12.** **Not making them want more.** The hook is clear and it's for them, but it opens no curiosity at all. Contrast solves it: what most people do or believe, next to a faster, simpler or unexpected way that works (step 3).

Beyond the spoken line, an assembly mistake also kills the hook: image, on-screen text and line about different things (item 3).

## 5. Step by step

### Step 1: one subject and one question

Each hook opens a single subject and a single question.

- Weak: "Today I'm going to talk about how I grew over the last year."
- Strong: "This is the simplest way for small business owners to get customers from their phone."

### Step 2: what appears in the first 2 to 3 seconds

- Show the object or the situation the video is about.
- Use a natural movement or a contrast: a push-in, a gesture or a change of scene. No chaos.
- An abstract subject calls for a concrete example, a striking photo or a quick demonstration.

The format of the video brief decides what can be shown ([formats](formats/README.md)).

### Step 3: the spoken line

One to three short, direct sentences.

- **Sentence 1:** the subject and the benefit, or the pain, in the first words.
- **Sentence 2:** a broken expectation or a contrast. "Most people try to solve [problem] by doing X, but what works is Y." The distance between what the viewer already knows and the new way creates curiosity.

That contrast states a fact: what most people do and what solves it, with Y stated directly. The binary contrast that denies one thing to assert another stays out ([no AI-speak](no-ai-speak.md)).

Check the line on four points:

- **Subject:** clear in the first words. The listener knows what it's about before the sentence ends.
- **What's at stake:** the result the viewer wants, like saving time, making money or getting rid of an annoyance.
- **Few words:** the smallest number of words that says everything, spoken at a normal pace. Cutting words helps; talking faster hurts.
- **Total clarity:** no double meaning, no confusing pun and no flourish.

### Step 4: the on-screen text

In the first 3 seconds:

- 3 to 5 keywords, in a large font, on up to two lines;
- the subject's name plus the promised result or the relief of the pain (example: "How to sleep better in 5 minutes");
- no unnecessary punctuation and no linking words ("therefore", "but");
- about the same thing as the spoken line and the image, ideally with movement or contrast in the scene.

### Step 5: the alignment test

- **With no sound:** can you understand the subject from the image and the on-screen text alone?
- **Only listening:** does the spoken line match what you see?

If either answer is no, fix it before showing it.

### Step 6: value in the next second

Right after the hook, the video delivers the promised tip or answer, without stalling and without changing the subject. When the structure of the video brief saves the whole answer for the end (a curiosity gap), the next second delivers a concrete piece of it, so the viewer feels it's worth staying.

That second opens the hold zone: the 5 to 10 seconds after the hook, in which the viewer checks whether the promise is real and whether they can trust the speaker. When choosing the hook, already think about what comes right after: a promise the video doesn't confirm within seconds loses the viewer. The step by step for that stretch is in [the hold zone](hold-zone.md).

## 6. How this piece talks to the rest of the method

- **Belief shock** ([hooks that work](knowledge/hooks-that-work.md)): it is still Hook 1, and it also answers the two questions. The contradicted belief says the subject; the desire says the gain.
- **The slide** ([breaking the bubble](breaking-the-bubble.md)): the hook is the opening, with identification or conflict, in the bubble's words. The hold zone pays the hook's promise before or during the transition to the common ground.
- **A comment that opens the video:** a conflict or provocation comment with many likes can open, as long as the subject appears in the same sentence or the next.
- **First person:** only with the subject clear in the same sentence. "Posting every day was the worst advice I ever got" works, because the subject shows up. "Look what happened to me" stays out.
- **Immersive scene and consequence before cause** (types in [hooks that work](knowledge/hooks-that-work.md)): the context can come later. The subject comes right away.
- **Format:** the format of the video brief decides the hook type and what can be shown in the opening.

## 7. How to show each option

```
Hook 1: [the spoken line, 1 to 3 short sentences]
On-screen text: [3 to 5 words, up to 2 lines]
Opening: [what appears in the first 2 to 3 seconds, and the movement]
Subject and gain: [what it's about] / [what the viewer gets]
Uses: [principles and addictive elements]
Why it works: [one line]
```

Each option passes the alignment test (step 5) before it appears.

In the script version, the "Hook" line carries only the spoken line, because that is what goes on the teleprompter. The opening and the on-screen text of the chosen hook go in a separate "How it opens" line.

## 8. Before showing

- **HK1.** In 2 seconds you can tell what the video is about and what you get.
- **HK2.** One subject and one question, with no greeting, introduction or detour.
- **HK3.** The spoken line has 1 to 3 short sentences, in the active voice, with the subject in the first words and what's at stake.
- **HK4.** The on-screen text has 3 to 5 words, on up to two lines.
- **HK5.** Image, text and spoken line are about the same thing (with no sound and only listening).
- **HK6.** The problem is said with "you".
- **HK7.** A concrete contrast makes the viewer want more.
- **HK8.** The next second delivers value and opens the hold zone ([hold zone](hold-zone.md)).

If the Sofia tools are available, run `check_script` on the hook options before showing them and check each point it returns in context (it can be a false positive). Its 15-word limit on the first sentence is a tool reference, not a rule of the method: the method asks for a hook of 1 to 3 short sentences.
