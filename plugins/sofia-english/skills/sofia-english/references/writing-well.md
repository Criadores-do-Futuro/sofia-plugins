# How to write scripts that sound natural

Two parts. Part 1 holds the rules that came from corrections to Sofia's scripts. Part 2 holds patterns taken from real viral scripts. [The Floor](floor.md) sits above both.

The examples are here to teach the WAY of writing, never to copy sentences. Invented examples carry {variables}, only to show the shape. Copy the swap, never the sentences. A real number only goes in with a source. Lines marked "from analyzed scripts" are English renderings of lines from Portuguese-language videos the method studied; they show the mechanism, not facts to reuse.

# Part 1: the house rules

Each rule comes with its reason, so you can apply it to cases the list doesn't cover.

**Low mental load.** Short sentences, one idea per sentence, words people use every day. The viewer is scrolling the feed: if they have to stop to decode it, they move on to the next video.

**Context before the name.** Say who someone is before saying the name: "the owner of the corner bakery, Rose" instead of "Rose". A bare name makes the viewer search their memory, and they get lost.

**Say what happened, in detail.** Who did what, how much, what for, when. "The city took action" leaves the viewer in the dark. "The city shut down the market and gave its 40 vendors 30 days to leave" shows the scene (invented example).

**A number becomes a picture.** A big number on its own says nothing. Compare it with something the viewer knows, using only math done from verified data.

**Open, hold and pay off the curiosity.** The hook opens a question. Each block answers a piece and opens the next. The ending pays the hook's promise. A promise with no payoff feels like a trick.

**The hook leads somewhere.** Keep the part the creator liked and take care of the end of the sentence, where hooks usually get lost. A good hook is concrete and leaves a question: who, how much, how.

**A short video still needs a story.** Short means fewer words, and every block still has a scene, a detail and a turn. A script that turns into a bullet list loses the viewer halfway. Write a real script, as long as the story needs, and check with the creator whether they have a length in mind.

**Change the subject without the viewer noticing.** To move to the next block, start from a concrete piece of the previous one: a word, an object, a number. An announced transition ("now let's talk about") breaks the rhythm.

**A caveat that flows.** When a fact needs care, the caveat goes inside the story, in the speaker's voice: "That's what the company says. The police are still investigating." A legal disclaimer pasted in the middle cuts the video dead.

**No accidental doubt.** Doubt words, like "it seems that" or "supposedly", make the viewer doubt everything. Use them only where the doubt is real, once, saying who makes the claim.

**One emotion per block.** Before writing each block, know what the viewer should feel there: anger, relief, surprise, the urge to comment.

**An addictive element happens in the sentence.** Saying in the idea that a block uses contrast helps planning. In the script, the viewer never hears the element's name: they feel two opposite things side by side, or they're left with a question in their head. If you can only point to the element by its label, it isn't in the text yet.

**Written to be spoken.** Read it out loud in your head. If you stumble or need to breathe in the middle, the sentence is too long.

**Fact, accusation and opinion kept apart.** An accusation is not yet a conviction: say who is accusing. If the fact changed later (the room that was locked was opened and found empty), tell the change. Someone's motive, money or use of AI only goes in with a source.

# Part 2: patterns from good scripts

## 1. Write the way the creator talks, not the way they write

Good scripts sound like conversation, not like an essay.

**Yes (from analyzed scripts):**
- "And that's how this lying businessman made billions."
- "Now stop and think."
- "And the worst part, the driver still has the nerve to..."
- "Nobody knew who Tommy Hilfiger was."

**No:**
- "In this video, we will analyze how a businessman achieved success."
- "Reflect upon the following."
- "Furthermore, the driver had the audacity to..."

**Rule:** Read the sentence out loud. If you would never say it like that in real life, rewrite it.

## 2. Short sentences that hit hard

Each sentence does ONE thing. Don't try to do three things in the same sentence.

**Yes (from analyzed scripts):**
- "The problem is simple: Tommy was a nobody."
- "And he didn't deserve to be on that list."
- "Overnight, everybody wanted to know who the hell Tommy Hilfiger was."

**No:**
- "The problem is that Tommy was a complete unknown and didn't deserve to be on that list, which made everyone ask who he was overnight."

**Rule:** If a sentence has more than 20 words, you can almost always split it in two.

## 3. Use "And", "So", "But" to start sentences

In formal writing that's wrong. In a script, it's what gives natural rhythm.

**Examples (from analyzed scripts):**
- "**And** suddenly, a car runs the red light."
- "**So** he says: I've done this more than 600 times."
- "**But** you, who actually deliver value, are left waiting."
- "**And** the worst part, the driver still..."

## 4. Narrate the scene instead of describing it

Don't say "he was persistent". Show him doing the thing 600 times.

**Yes (shows, from analyzed scripts):**
- "He drives calmly, checks the lights, follows the rules of the road."
- "Inside that car there's a couple laughing, smoking, filming stories."
- "I've done this more than 600 times."

**No (tells):**
- "He was a careful and responsible driver."
- "The occupants of the other vehicle were irresponsible."
- "He had a lot of experience with the recipe."

**Rule:** Swap adjectives for actions. "Brave" → show what he did. "Reckless" → show the reckless thing he did.

## 5. "But" is the strongest tool in the script

"But" creates contrast on the spot. It turns the direction. It breaks expectation. Each "but" is a small turn, and many turns hold attention.

**Examples (from analyzed scripts):**
- "Today it brings in 9 billion, **but** back then it was nothing."
- "You've never heard of them, **but** they're the reference for thousands."
- "The irony is that bad professionals do this every day. **But** you, who actually deliver value..."

**Three ways to build it** (with {variables}):
- Positive build, "but", reversal: "Today it brings in {sourced amount}, but at the start {the situation before}."
- Negative build, "but", surprise: "You've never heard of them, but {sourced fact}."
- What the audience thinks, "but", the opposite: "You deliver value, but you're left waiting for recognition."

**Rule:** When you want to turn the story's direction, use "but". When you want to intensify, use "and".

## 6. Questions only in the final CTA or when they came from a comment

Good scripts put what the audience is already thinking into the spoken line, as a statement. The rhetorical question, asked only to be answered right away, stays out of the script.

Two questions stay:
- the one in the final call to action, which expects the viewer's answer in the comments;
- the one that came from an audience comment and became the creator's line, with the comment's words.

**Rule:** If the audience would be thinking it at that moment, say it as a statement. "Who the hell is Tommy Hilfiger?" becomes "Nobody knew who Tommy Hilfiger was."

## 7. Specific beats generic

"8 square meters in São Paulo" > "a small apartment"
"600 times" > "a lot of times"
"9 billion dollars" > "a lot of money"
"200 thousand dollars" > "not much money"

(The first four come from analyzed scripts; each number had its source in the original video.)

- A proper name instead of the category ("Taylor Swift" instead of "a famous singer").
- An exact number instead of "many times", always with a source ("{sourced number} times").
- An odd detail instead of the explanation ("her second cousin" instead of "a relative").
- A scene instead of the feeling ("got up early, exhausted, just to drive you" instead of "he was dedicated").

Why it works, in the method's observation: the viewer can't picture "a famous person", and can picture someone with a name. Whoever pictures the scene stays. The more specific, the more real it feels, and the more the viewer believes it.

**Rule:** Concrete numbers, place names, visual details. Swap the generic adjective for a scene. Where you wrote "many", put the sourced number, or cut the sentence. Where you wrote "a bad feeling", describe the scene that causes the feeling.

## 8. The emotional staircase: each sentence climbs a step

Don't throw everything at once. Each sentence intensifies the previous one: fact, consequence, bigger consequence, peak.

**Example (from an analyzed script about a car crash):**
1. "Dad got up early" (tenderness)
2. "He drives calmly" (calm)
3. "A car runs the light" (shock)
4. "A couple laughing, smoking, filming stories" (anger)
5. "It hits yours and puts your dad's life at risk" (fear and intense anger)
6. "The driver gets out yelling" (peak outrage)
7. "And these are the people getting applause" (revolt)

Each step sits above the previous one. It never goes down. Two sentences in a row on the same level call for a cut.

Invented example: if the first sentence is "he lied", the next can't be "he deceived" (same level). The next climbs: "and the lie became a {sourced amount} brand".

## 9. Someone else's story becomes a mirror

The story can be about someone else, but the ending has to be about THE VIEWER.

- Tell someone's story (a famous person, a character, a real case).
- At the right moment, turn the camera on the audience: "and the same thing happens to you".
- Show that the viewer's life has the same pattern.

**Examples (from analyzed scripts):**
- "Don't confuse arrogance with confidence." (goes from the MasterChef story to YOUR life)
- "Share this with someone who needs to see it." (turns the viewer into a participant)
- "You, who actually deliver value, are left waiting." (leaves Tommy and comes to YOU)

Never end a story without bringing it into the viewer's life. What the audience wants to know about is themselves. The story is the road. The moral applied to their life is the destination.

**Rule:** The last third of the script leaves the character and comes to the audience.

## 10. Words that create pictures: real words

The words that work in a script create a picture in the head.

**Words that create pictures (from analyzed scripts):**
- "laughing, smoking, filming stories" (you see the scene)
- "got up early even though he was tired" (you picture the dad)
- "a giant billboard in Times Square" (you can see it)
- "trying to go after your dad" (you feel the threat)

**Words that DON'T create pictures:**
- "inappropriate behavior"
- "irresponsible individuals"
- "advertising investment"
- "aggressive attitude"

Use real words: everyday ones, even the "ugly" ones; the slang the audience recognizes; the terms the creator would use telling the story at a bar. Examples: "this guy" instead of "this individual"; "go after him" instead of "confront him"; "I got the idea of my life in the bathroom" instead of "I had an epiphany". If the "correct" word sounds like an essay, it's the wrong one.

**Rule:** Action verbs + sensory details > abstract nouns + generic adjectives.

## 11. A short sentence after a long one

Good scripts alternate sentences that build context with short sentences that stop everything.

- Long context, then "Nothing." (one word).
- Long context, then "Nobody answered." (two words).
- Long context, then "From the artist I never became." (a fragment with weight).

In practice: after building the idea in 2 or 3 sentences, deliver the impact in a short sentence. It can be a fragment, a contradiction or a reveal. A 2- or 3-word sentence carries weight.

## 12. Sentences that make the viewer see

- "You know that person who {specific behavior}." The viewer remembers someone and sees a face.
- "Picture this: {place}, {time}, {action}." The viewer builds the image.
- "Think about the last time you {everyday situation}." The viewer connects the subject to their own life.

Describe the behavior instead of the personality: "super polite to your face, trash-talks the team behind their back" instead of "a fake person".

## 13. A light ending after a heavy story

The more elaborate the story, the simpler the close.

- A complex, emotional story, then a final line with no pretension.
- An absurd build-up, then "anyway, that's how {something simple}".
- A moment of weakness, then a light joke at your own expense.

In the method's observation, the distance between the weight of the content and the lightness of the ending makes people laugh, brings relief and creates identification. Close like someone telling a story at a bar: "and that was it". The self-deprecating joke works when it comes after confidence.

## 14. Contradiction in the opening

Two pieces of information that don't fit together make the viewer keep going to understand.

- "Couples therapy. Without being a real couple."
- "He lied to everyone and got rich doing it." (lying and success don't fit)
- "I don't want to be seen. I'm a total show-off."

Contradiction works best when both parts are short and back to back.

## 15. A CTA that matches the video

- A CTA that continues the video's subject.
- A trade: ask for something simple and offer something in return.
- Or no CTA, when the content is enough.

If the video is about being authentic, the CTA can be "follow me because I talk about what I love". If the video taught something, "comment {word} and I'll send you {material}". A loose "like and share", with no tie to the topic, goes.

## Golden rule

Copy the emotion and the logic. The words are always new: from the creator's subject, their audience and the real stories they bring.
