# Analyzed examples: Storytelling about a person

The quoted script lines below are translated from the Portuguese original. Names, years and numbers are the original creator's claims, unchanged; check them in a primary source before reusing any of them (see [facts and sources](../facts-and-sources.md)). Learn the shape, never copy the lines.

## Example 1: Tommy Hilfiger (the "lying" businessman)

**Format:** Direct to camera, narrating a story (see [direct to camera](../formats/direct-to-camera.md))
**Structure:** Villain → Reveal → Connected moral

---

> "And that's how this lying businessman made billions."

(Universal conflict + Curiosity) "Lying" sparks anger, "billions" contradicts it: the viewer wants to understand how.

> "In 1985, an unknown young guy named Tommy Hilfiger had a failing store."

(Contrast + Familiarity) A billionaire who starts out broke. Tommy is a known brand, so it lands fast.

> "He didn't have the money to compete with the giants, so he decided to do something nobody expected: lie shamelessly to the whole world."

(Curiosity + Conflict/Change) The lack of money forces the decision. "Lie shamelessly" raises the curiosity.

> "Tommy hired the ad man George Lewis to create a campaign with only 200 thousand dollars."

(Mental debate) "Only 200 thousand": a lot to some, nothing to others. The viewer is already forming an opinion.

> "Lewis put up a giant billboard in Times Square with the names of the four greatest American designers: Ralph Lauren, Calvin Klein, Perry Ellis and Tommy Hilfiger."

(Aha) The strategy revealed. "Times Square" creates an image. His name next to the greats: audacity.

> "The problem? Tommy was a total unknown and didn't deserve to be on that list."

(Contrast) A short sentence that hits. "The problem?" is a rhetorical question the audience was already asking.

> "Overnight, the question everyone was asking was: 'Who the hell is Tommy Hilfiger?'"

(Curiosity) "Who the hell" is real speech. It voices what the audience thinks.

> "The campaign set off an avalanche of national publicity within days. He literally declared himself big before he was big."

(Change) The consequence of the lie. "Declaring yourself big before you're big" creates desire in the audience.

> "Today, Tommy Hilfiger brings in over 9 billion dollars, but back then it was nothing."

(Contrast) "9 billion": a specific number adds weight. The "but" turns the direction.

> "The irony is that bad professionals do this every day."

(Moral begins) Leaves the character, turns to the audience. A mirror.

> "But you, who actually deliver value, keep waiting for someone to recognize your worth."

(Identification + Contrast) The viewer sees themselves here. "They grow by lying, you stay invisible."

> "While you're modest about your real ability, they're bold about their incompetence."

(Contrast) A symmetrical line that sticks.

> "This isn't about lying, it's about revealing your true potential."

(Aha + Moral) Changes the meaning of the whole story. It wasn't a lie, it was confidence.

> "Tommy surely already knew he was good. He just wasn't known. Share this with someone who needs to see this video."

A natural CTA that flows from the moral.

---

### Patterns to learn
1. Opening with a negative label + a positive result = a curious contradiction
2. Specific numbers instead of "a lot" or "a little"
3. The last third leaves the character and turns to the audience
4. The CTA as a consequence, not a request

### What today's method would change
This script predates the current rules. Keep the mechanics, but in a new script: "The problem?" becomes a statement ("Tommy was a total unknown."), since the Floor removes rhetorical questions; "This isn't about lying, it's about..." becomes a direct statement of the second half, since the "not X, it's Y" template is out; "literally," "actually" and "surely" get cut (see [the Floor](../floor.md) and [no AI-speak](../no-ai-speak.md)).
