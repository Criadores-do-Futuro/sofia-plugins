# Format: Narrator

A character on screen and an off-screen narrator.

## What it is
An off-screen voice introduces or provokes the visible character, who reacts, agrees, disagrees or finishes the narration. The narrator never appears on screen; the fun comes from the interaction between the voice and the character.

## Audio and visual signature
- Two clearly distinct voices or roles
- A visible person reacts to a voice with no visible owner
- Narrative back-and-forth, not just a voice over B-roll
- Captions can tell narrator and character apart

## Best-fit structure
A short dramatic [Dialogue](dialogue.md) or [ISC](../structures/identification-story-content.md) with tension between narrator and character.

## Hook type this format asks for
- **FM.narrator.H.** An unexpected contrast: the narrator says something and the character pushes back, right in the first exchange.

## Dominant addictive elements
[Conflict and change](../addictive-elements/conflict-and-change.md), [contrast](../addictive-elements/contrast.md), [familiar language](../addictive-elements/familiar-language.md).

## Writing rules
- **FM.narrator.1.** Give the narrator a clear job: introduce, expose, pressure or contradict.
- **FM.narrator.2.** Make the character react visibly and verbally; don't write two parallel monologues.
- **FM.narrator.3.** Alternate short lines to keep rhythm and cause and effect.
- **FM.narrator.4.** Let the narrator know or reveal something that puts pressure on the character.
- **FM.narrator.5.** Escalate the interaction to a decision, a confession or a turn, with a clear resolution.
- **FM.narrator.6.** Tell the voices apart by vocabulary, intention and captions, not just by tone of voice.

## Don't confuse with
- [Voice-Over](voice-over.md): a voice over images or B-roll with no character reacting.
- [Dialogue](dialogue.md): both people in the conversation appear, or are characters on screen.
- Two independent voices: without interaction, it isn't the Narrator format.
