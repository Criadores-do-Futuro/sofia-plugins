# Spoken writing and factual integrity

Write to one person using the creator's actual vocabulary. With no sample, use plain American English, natural contractions and no forced slang. Vary sentence length according to thought and breath. Keep spoken lines separate from visual directions and editorial notes.

Each line should add a fact, image, question, consequence, turn or action. Remove filler and repeated explanations. Prefer an actor doing something concrete to abstract praise. Replace generic claims with supported details, not stronger adjectives.

A useful opening contains a specific payload and a question worth answering. Contrast and curiosity can help; do not require a fixed number of triggers or use a shocking statistic you cannot verify. The ending delivers the exact promise, then asks for an action that fits the user's objective.

Avoid formulaic slogans, empty superlatives, decorative metaphors and automatic transitions. A real creator's speech can include repetition, longer sentences or unusual words; preserve those when they serve the idea. These preferences never justify changing a fact.

For every external factual claim, keep a source opened in this session. Attribute the creator's facts to them. Mark an unavailable fact as `[REAL FACT NEEDED]`; explain what is needed separately. Label a teaching illustration as an illustration and never present it as a real customer story, quote or result.

Before delivery, read the lines for natural speech, check the payoff, confirm all factual support and remove unsupported performance predictions. Only say a check passed when you actually performed it.
