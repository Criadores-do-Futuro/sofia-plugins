# Analysis structure

**Conflict → Results → 3 reasons → Moral.**

In practice, in blocks: Opening clip of the subject > Exaggerated label > Credential > Promise > Principle 1 > Principle 2 > Principle 3 (the pillar of your method) > Turn to the viewer > Final ask.

## Overview

You take someone the audience has already seen in their feed, borrow that person's attention, explain in three principles why they work, and use the last principle as a bridge to what you teach or sell. The subject is the proof; the method is the product.

**Fixed roles:**
- **The subject** is an outside person who entertains: a creator, a brand, an athlete. Never the profile of the person recording. The funny or shocking lines (the punchlines) come out of the subject's mouth, in short clips.
- **The presenter** is a fan and a teacher: announces, reacts, names the mechanism and pulls out the lesson. Never the topic: only talks about themselves in the close.
- **The viewer** gets the lesson, even when the subject is a comedian.

**Measures from the 13 reference videos the method analyzed** (an observation of the method, not a fixed rule): 115 to 180 seconds, 440 to 640 words (about 3.5 to 4 words per second, measured in Portuguese). In the best ones, the subject's voice fills 8% to 16% of the time, in clips of 1 to 7 seconds, with the strong part at the end of the clip.

## Steps

### 1. Conflict: opening clip and label (0 to 20 s)

**Opening clip (up to 4 s):** a line from the subject that makes sense on its own, with no context, with the strong part at the end. A mass-appeal theme: money, taxes, body, babies, jobs, jealousy. If no clip like that exists, pick another subject. Never open with the presenter talking jargon.

**Exaggerated label:** a fake attack that turns into praise. Right after it, the turn that shows it was praise. Two formulas:
- Superlative: "{This woman} has {the most Y X} on the internet, in my opinion."
- Affectionate insult: "This girl has {the most Z thing} on the internet."

Label rules:
- The joke is obvious right away. Nobody can think it's true.
- It's about something true and harmless in the subject's content (the way they talk, the catchphrase, the habit).
- Superlative only as the speaker's opinion ("to me, {she} has {the most Y X} on the internet") or with a number that has a source.
- Nothing that sounds like real news: no "is under investigation," no "was reported," no crime, illness or lawsuit.
- An affectionate insult is fine about something the subject owns. It never offends the viewer (what not to do: "Gen Z who hates working" insults the very audience).
- Profanity only from the subject's mouth, bleeped.

### 2. Results: name, credential and promise (10 to 26 s)

Spoken name, followers (rounded down, with "over," "almost" or "and counting"), average or peak views, and a detail that becomes a story ("just this year," "posts less than once a week," "works in 5 niches"). No guessed numbers.

- "Gained over {N} followers just this year. An average of {M} views."
- "{Name} has {N} followers, videos with almost {M} views, and posts less than {frequency}." (big, bigger, contrast)
- An open curiosity gap, if you want: "And a few invisible tricks that get you hooked on everything {she} posts."

Avoid: a credential without followers, a niche-scale number ("the coolest among realtors"), a guessed number, metrics only in likes.

**Promise (between 19 and 26 s), with the subject as the grammatical subject:** "That's why today we're going to look at the three principles {name} uses to {result}." "That's why" ties credential to promise; "three" sets up a countdown. Too early (4 s) doesn't create desire; too late (30 s) loses people. Never "I'm going to use the analysis format."

### 3. The 3 reasons: the principles (25 to 40 s each)

Each principle, in this order:
1. **Name**, short (2 to 5 words), with the number spoken ("Number one...", "Two...", "And three..."). At least one name comes from the subject, like a nickname for their format (in the Portuguese reference videos: a nickname borrowed from an old Brazilian educational TV show, "get ready with me," a joke nickname for the subject). In the reference videos, the weakest ones used a textbook term alone ("storytelling," "authenticity").
2. **Proof:** a list of three facts, the third breaking the expectation in a funny way, plus a clip of up to 7 s where the principle happens on its own. The presenter sets up, the subject delivers the punchline, the presenter reacts.
3. **Mechanism:** why it hooks ("the human mind is addicted to...", "what I call..."), with the source when there is one (Hebb 1949, McKee, Loewenstein).
4. **Lesson for "you,"** in the imperative: "So stop talking only about your product and connect it to...". A descriptive lesson ("he does this") is weaker.
5. **Bridge** to the next principle: "Which connects to pillar number 2."

The third principle is the pillar of your method or product (in the 13 reference videos: "creative format: find a pattern that works and repeat, repeat, repeat... and that's how {name} and the biggest creators in the country {result}"). That way the subject becomes living proof of what you teach. If the pillar comes in the first principle, the bridge to the final ask disappears.

One breather in the whole video, right after an exaggeration: "Just kidding, don't want to make it heavy."

Templates that work in the body:
- Inversion with a common villain: "It might look like just {surface reading}, but {name} has more {real quality} than most {pretentious group} who {hypocrisy}."
- Contrast: "The same guy who {three serious things} is the same guy who {three silly things}."
- Ladder: "When content is good, you think {1}. When it's on another level, it makes you feel {2}."
- Ups and downs: "We saw {her} {at a low}. Saw {at a high}. But {loss with a number}. And we also saw {the comeback}."
- Predictions: "When {name} shows up {doing the format}, we already know three things. First... Second... And finally..."
- Chain that ends in money: "Sharing {X} makes people trust you. Root for you. And buy what you sell."
- Meta: "Like me right here, {using the principle right now} to teach you without you noticing."

### 4. Moral of the story: turn to the viewer and final ask

**Turn (6 to 13 s):** the analysis becomes a diagnosis. A direct diagnosis ("Your content is missing {pillar}."), a scolding ("It makes no sense! You're creating content in {year} without...") or the audience's objection acted out ("Ugh, {your name}, but it's so hard for me...") followed by the answer ("That's how I felt too. But after watching the biggest creators...").

**Final ask (3 to 14 s),** strongest to weakest:
- Back to the subject: "Too bad {name} doesn't teach {what you sell}. But I do. Want to learn with me? Just tap the link in my bio."
- Social proof ladder with the offer: "Over the last few weeks I've helped {number with source} people hit 10, 30, 50 thousand followers using this strategy."
- The humble version: "That's the format that works for her. If you want help finding yours..."
- Weak: a flat ask, with no turn, or for something the audience doesn't know.

## Minimum research before writing

- Clips: 3 to 5 opening candidates (up to 4 s each, understandable on their own) and one proof clip per principle, with timestamps.
- Numbers: followers, peak and average views, recent growth, posting frequency, visible earnings from the content.
- The subject's story: origin, a fall with a number, the comeback, a recent personal event, the villain or the contrast.
- Format: the pattern that repeats in 5 or more videos, with a nickname (it becomes a principle's name); catchphrases; who always appears; themes in lists of three.
- Audience: the nicknames and jokes in the comments, the mistake the audience makes (it becomes the scolding) and their objection (it becomes the acted-out objection).

Every number and fact follows [facts and sources](../facts-and-sources.md).

**Screening the subject:** they need a clip of up to 4 s that gets a laugh or a shock with no context, a follower count that impresses in one sentence and a theme the audience feels strongly. If the niche is narrow, translate it into a human emotion or switch subjects.

It also works for a brand (opening = the most absurd commercial; credential = revenue or store count) and for an athlete or public figure (opening = the play or the line that went viral; credential = titles or records). What doesn't change: the subject delivers the entertainment, the presenter names the mechanism, the viewer gets the lesson and the last principle bridges to what is sold.

What Analysis asks for: an opening clip that makes sense on its own; a funny label that doesn't offend; followers, views and a detail before the promise; the promise between 19 and 26 s with the subject as the grammatical subject; three principles with name, proof, mechanism, lesson and bridge; one name taken from the subject; the last principle is your pillar; the presenter only talks about themselves at the end; a turn before the ask.

A worked example is in [the analysis example](../examples/analysis.md).

## Goals of the structure

- Ride the attention of someone who is already going viral
- Build authority by analyzing other people's success
- Plant the pillars of your method without sounding like a class
- Sell with the subject as living proof

## When to use it

- When a creator, brand or video went viral and you want to ride the trend
- When you want to teach your pillars indirectly
- "Why did so-and-so grow so much" or "the secret behind X" videos

## Quick checklist

- **ST.analysis.1.** Is the hook a clip of the subject that makes sense on its own in up to 4 s?
- **ST.analysis.2.** Is the label exaggerated, funny and inoffensive to the viewer?
- **ST.analysis.3.** Followers + views + a unique detail, before the promise?
- **ST.analysis.4.** Does the promise land between 19 and 26 s, with the **subject** as the grammatical subject?
- **ST.analysis.5.** Three principles, each with name → proof → mechanism → lesson → bridge?
- **ST.analysis.6.** At least one principle named after something from the subject?
- **ST.analysis.7.** Is the last principle the pillar of your method or product?
- **ST.analysis.8.** Does the presenter only talk about themselves in the close?
- **ST.analysis.9.** A turn to the viewer before the CTA?
- **ST.analysis.10.** Do the subject's clips add up to 8–16% of the time, each up to 7 s, punchline at the end?
- **ST.analysis.11.** Profanity only from the subject's mouth, and none on sensitive topics?
