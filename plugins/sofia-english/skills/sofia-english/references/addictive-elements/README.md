# Addictive elements

They are what makes a viewer stop, stay and comment. They work in any format and any structure. Every idea says which elements it uses and where each one appears. Each element has its own piece (`addictive-elements/<id>`), with the principle, examples and mistakes.

The examples in the element pieces are internal references, only to show the shape. They don't go into the conversation with the creator or into the script. The principle is what guides writing and analysis.

## Hierarchy

This order applies only to the addictive elements, when choosing what goes into a script. The method's principles ([principles](../principles.md)) have their own order, which is for judging why a video holds attention. The two lists use similar names and serve different jobs.

| Order | Element | Piece | Without it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Theme and moral | [theme-and-moral](theme-and-moral.md) | Nobody stops, and nothing sticks |
| 2 | Emotional relevance | [emotional-relevance](emotional-relevance.md) | There is no attention |
| 3 | Conflict and change | [conflict-and-change](conflict-and-change.md) | There is no story |
| 4 | Curiosity and "aha" | [curiosity-and-aha](curiosity-and-aha.md) | Nobody stays to the end, and nobody comes back |
| 5 | Contrast | [contrast](contrast.md) | Everything feels lukewarm |
| 6 | Familiar language and mental image | [familiar-language](familiar-language.md) | The viewer gives up on understanding |

The elements work together. Every block of the script has at least one active element.

## Curiosity with "aha"

| Combination | Result |
|---|---|
| Curiosity without "aha," by forgetting | Clickbait. The viewer feels tricked |
| "Aha" without curiosity | Useful information nobody stops to hear |
| Curiosity with "aha" | Holds and rewards |

**A question left open on purpose.** The method accepts withholding the "aha" at the end when the doubt is the moral (an unsolved case, a debate the audience will settle in the comments). The difference is intent: a question left open on purpose is a choice, and Sofia tells the creator she made that choice and why. A forgotten question is clickbait. In both cases, the hook's promise is still paid.

## The six elements, briefly

The examples in this section are invented, only to show the shape. Each element's piece has the rest.

### Theme and moral

The **theme** is what grabs attention: the way in. The **moral** is the idea you want to plant: the destination. The theme is the vehicle, the moral is the cargo.

The most common mistake is using the technical subject as the theme. "Owner's draw" makes nobody stop. A conflict everyone recognizes (money in a relationship, betrayal, injustice) makes people stop, and leads to the moral, which can be the technical content.

**Good theme:** sparks a feeling, divides opinion, the audience has lived it, and it can bridge to the moral.
**Good moral:** specific, clear, and comes out of the theme without forcing.

Weak: "Today I'm going to talk about money management." Strong: "She paid every bill in the house and he called it a partnership." (theme: money in a couple; moral: keep your money separate from the business's)

Mistakes: a technical theme; a good theme with no moral (entertains and plants nothing); a good moral with a weak theme (nobody stops); a moral disconnected from the theme.

### Emotional relevance

Without emotion there is no attention. Pick a theme that touches a feeling the audience knows, and make the first sentence spark that feeling.

Emotions that work: outrage (something unfair), curiosity (something to discover), identification (something already lived), hope (something wanted), fear (something to avoid), humor.

Signal in the comments: people telling their own story, agreeing or disagreeing hard, arguing with each other.

Mistakes: a theme that moves nobody; emotion only at the start and the rest lukewarm; an emotion that doesn't belong to the audience's bubble.

### Conflict and change

No conflict, no story. Conflict is a problem big enough to force someone to act. After it, something changes: conflict, action, change.

- **Universal conflict:** death, betrayal, injustice, abandonment, humiliation.
- **Everyday conflict:** fighting with the boss, missing the bus, being ignored, messing up in public.
- **Chained:** each change opens a new conflict. The viewer can't let go, because every answer brings another problem.

Weak: "He worked at a store and later opened his own." Strong: "The store owner fired Jake by text, on a Friday. By Monday, Jake was selling the same stuff on the sidewalk across the street."

Mistakes: only describing facts; conflict without change (frustrating); weak conflict; a change that doesn't match the conflict.

### Curiosity and the "aha" effect

**Curiosity** opens a question in the head and holds the answer. **"Aha"** is the moment the answer arrives and the viewer thinks "now I get it."

Ways to open curiosity:
- State something surprising that makes the viewer ask "wait, what?"
- Give part of the answer and hold the rest.
- Put together two things that don't fit ("his mistake paid for the new house").
- Announce that something will be revealed, and reveal it.

How to apply it: open in the hook, hold, raise the urge to know with details, close with a clear answer. There can be several cycles in one script, one per block. The combination of curiosity with "aha," and the question left open on purpose, are in the section "Curiosity with "aha"" above.

Mistakes: giving the answer away early; promising a lot and delivering little; opening several questions and closing none.

### Contrast

We only notice heat because cold exists. Putting opposites side by side makes the feeling stronger and gets the audience to take sides in the comments.

- **Of emotion:** alternating praise and criticism, love and anger.
- **Visual:** before and after, full and empty, alone and together.
- **Of expectation:** starts looking like criticism and turns into praise.
- **Of words:** calling the same thing a "failure" and later "the best thing that ever happened to me."

How to apply it: find the two poles of the theme, alternate between them for rhythm and, at the end, make your position clear.

Mistakes: contrast with no purpose; staying on one pole; leaving the contrast unresolved at the end.

### Familiar language and mental image

The mind spends as little energy as it can. A hard word makes the viewer give up. An everyday word goes in effortlessly.

**Familiar language:** the same words, slang and references the audience uses. Write like you're explaining to a friend.
**Mental image:** the viewer sees the scene just by hearing it. "Folding chair at a cookout" already brings the whole backyard.

The two go together: without a familiar word there is no image.

Weak: "You need consistency in your actions." Strong: "It's doing it every day, like brushing your teeth."

How to apply it: swap abstraction for a scene, a technical term for an everyday comparison, and test whether someone outside the niche understands.

The check for these elements in a script is in "Before delivering," at the end of this piece.

## How Sofia uses the elements

- When writing, every stretch of the script has at least one active element.
- When analyzing a video, she marks which elements are in each sentence.
- When critiquing, she checks whether the elements were used and points out where they're missing.
- When improving a script, she rewrites the weak stretches with the missing elements.

## When analyzing a video

For each element, mark where it appears (the second or the sentence) and whether it worked. Note how many conflict-and-change pairs exist, where curiosity opens and how long it is held, whether the "aha" was worth the wait (or the question was left open on purpose), whether the comments show debate (a sign of contrast) and whether someone outside the niche would understand.

## Before delivering

- **AE1.** The theme makes people stop and the moral comes out of it without forcing.
- **AE2.** The first sentence sparks a feeling the bubble knows, and the emotion doesn't die after the start.
- **AE3.** There is conflict with change, and the change matches the conflict.
- **AE4.** Curiosity opens in the hook and closes with a clear "aha," or stays open on purpose, with the choice told to the creator.
- **AE5.** The contrast has a purpose and resolves at the end.
- **AE6.** The words are the audience's everyday words and create images; someone outside the niche understands.
- **AE7.** Every block has at least one active element, and the format's dominant elements appear.
