# Familiar language and mental image

**The mind is lazy: it doesn't like spending energy. Talk the way your audience talks.**

> IMPORTANT: The examples in this piece are internal references that illustrate the principle. Do NOT use them in conversations with the creator or reproduce them in scripts. The PRINCIPLE guides writing and analysis, not the examples.

## The principle: familiar language

If you talk fancy, the brain feels it has to spend a lot of energy to understand, and it gives up.

If you talk in a familiar way, with words the viewer already uses every day, they don't have to work to understand.

**Familiar language means using the same words, expressions and references your audience uses in daily life.**

### How it works

- Swap technical terms for everyday comparisons
- Use phrases the audience already says and hears
- Write like you're explaining to a friend
- Even complex topics get easy when explained in everyday language

### The principle in action (internal reference)

- Instead of "consistency in your actions" → "Doing it every day, like brushing your teeth"
- Instead of "technical features of athletic footwear" → "Air conditioning for your feet"
- Neuroscience explained with "eating a salad instead of a burger" and "a kid crying to avoid brushing their teeth"

## The principle: mental image

**It's when the viewer sees a scene in their head just from hearing you talk. The brain loves that.**

Say "folding chair at a cookout" and the viewer instantly pictures the whole backyard. That's a mental image.

### Connection with familiar language

To create a mental image, you HAVE to use familiar language. The two are always connected.

When the audience can "see" the scene you describe, they feel inside the story.

### How to create a mental image

1. Use objects, places and situations the audience knows
2. Describe scenes with concrete visual details
3. Make the viewer "see" the situation happening
4. Use references from the audience's daily life

### Signs the mental image is working

- The viewer can picture the described scene
- They feel inside the story
- They can "see" the characters and situations
- They feel the scene's emotions as if they were there

## How to apply it when writing scripts

1. **Know your audience's vocabulary:** which words, slang and expressions do they use?
2. **Swap abstractions for concrete scenes:** instead of "having discipline," describe the scene of someone doing the thing
3. **Use everyday comparisons:** compare complex concepts with simple things
4. **Test: can the viewer SEE what you're saying?** If not, rewrite with more visual detail

## How to apply it when analyzing videos

When analyzing a video, identify:

1. Does the creator use words the audience uses every day?
2. Are there comparisons that simplify concepts?
3. Can the audience "see" the described scenes?
4. Are there cultural references the audience recognizes?

## Checklist

- [ ] Does the script use language the target audience recognizes?
- [ ] Were technical terms swapped for simple comparisons?
- [ ] Do the described scenes create a clear mental image?
- [ ] Are the cultural references from the audience's bubble?
- [ ] Could someone outside the niche understand the text?
