# Hooks that work

A growing base of hooks collected from viral videos the method analyzed, with the hook types, the patterns and how to present the options. The hook is the first second of the video: the spoken line, the on-screen text and what appears, so the viewer stops scrolling. The foundation of every hook is in [hook](../hook.md): within 2 seconds, the viewer knows what the video is about and what they gain by staying, with image, text and spoken line telling the same thing. This piece covers the hook types.

The collected hooks below come from Brazilian videos. They're given as English renderings of the Portuguese originals, to show the mechanism. They aren't verified facts to reuse: any fact in a new hook needs its own source.

## What a good hook has

- **It's concrete.** It has a person, a number, an object, a place. The listener can see the scene.
- **It leaves a question in the listener's head.** Who, how much, how, what happened next. In the method's observation, "who", "how" and "how much" questions pay off more than "why". The hook leaves the question with a statement, without asking it out loud.
- **It's true.** Shock, numbers and news only with a source. An opinion counts when it's the speaker's sincere opinion and the video proves it.
- **The video pays it off.** Before suggesting a hook, point to the block where the script answers the hook's question. If the body doesn't prove it, the hook doesn't work.
- **It matches the format.** Each format calls for a type of opening (see the format's piece in [formats](../formats/README.md)).
- **It combines two or three elements.** In the method, the strongest hooks mix, for example, curiosity with something familiar, or conflict with a number.

## Hypothesis under test: the belief-shock hook (since 09/18/2026)

**The hypothesis:** the hook that holds best causes a **mental conflict**. It contradicts what the viewer believes **and** says something they wish were true. The result shocks.

**Reference examples:**
- "Stop posting on Instagram."
- "Posting every day on Instagram was the worst advice I ever got."
- "Man arrested for losing weight too fast."

**Why they work.**
- "Stop posting on Instagram": the belief is that you have to post to grow. The secret wish is to post less and still do well. The shock is an order that contradicts the rule everyone follows.
- "Posting every day was the worst advice": the belief is that daily consistency is mandatory. The secret wish is that it isn't. The shock comes from calling a "sacred" tip the worst.
- "Man arrested for losing weight too fast": the belief is that losing weight fast is good. The shock comes from an absurd consequence that opens the question "wait, what?".

**Formula.** [the belief the audience holds] → flipped inside out → the version they wish were true, said as an order, a confession or an absurd consequence.
- **Order:** "Stop [thing everyone does]." / "Delete [thing everyone keeps]." / "Cancel [thing everyone subscribes to]."
- **Confession:** "[Sacred rule] was the worst advice I ever got."
- **Absurd consequence:** "[Person] [punishment or loss] for [doing the thing everyone thinks is good]."

**Belief shock, step by step.**
1. Write in one line what the audience believes about the theme.
2. Write in one line what they wish were true (usually: less work, less cost, less guilt).
3. The hook states item 2 against item 1, in the fewest words, as an order, confession or absurd consequence.
4. Point to where the video pays off the shock. If the body of the script doesn't prove the statement, the hook doesn't work.
5. In every hook request, option 1 is always a belief shock. The others follow the types below, for comparison. If the creator profile shows another type works better with this audience, show the creator that alongside the options.

**Limits (they outrank the shock).**
- The shock has to be true, or the creator's sincere opinion proven in the video. No invented news: "man arrested" only works if it happened and has a source.
- A promise of results ("you'll grow", "you'll make money") stays out. An order to stop is fine.
- [No AI-speak](../no-ai-speak.md) still applies. The shock tends to pull toward the pattern that denies one thing to assert another: swap it for a direct order.

## Hook types

Every hook falls into one of these types. The writer picks the type before writing the line, and the right type depends on the recording format (the format's piece, section "Hook type this format calls for").

| Type | What it does | Template | Collected example (translated) | Formats that call for it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Belief shock | Contradicts what the audience believes and says what they wish were true | "Stop {thing everyone does}." / "{Sacred rule} was the worst advice I ever got." | "Stop posting on Instagram." | Direct to camera, Lo-fi, Off-camera conversation, Everyday conversation |
| Consequence statement | Delivers the result before the cause and leaves the question of how it happened | "And that's how {person} {surprising result}." | "And that's how this lying businessman made billions" | Behind the scenes, Split screen, Green screen, Visual storytelling, Voice-over, Fast-paced, Vlog |
| Direct provocation | Challenges a belief or habit of the viewer | "If you {common habit}, {consequence that hurts}." | "If your girlfriend sent you this video, it's because you need to learn a lesson" | Direct to camera, Question box, Lo-fi, Dialogue |
| Immersive scene | Puts the viewer inside a moment before any context | "{Place}, {time}. {Person} {action in the middle of the scene}." | "You can't save your parents" | Cinematic, Voice-over, Situational conflict, Analogy, Vlog, Split screen |
| Unexpected contrast | Puts together two things that shouldn't be together | "{Ordinary thing} {did something absurd} and {consequence in your life}." | "My mother-in-law's snake had surgery and is going to stay at my house" | Comparison, Dialogue, Narrator, The Office, Lazy creative, Analogy, Combined format |
| Numeric curiosity | A concrete data point that creates a question instantly | "{Known thing} has {verified number} of {something scary}." | "Coke Zero has 36 milligrams of aspartame" | Fast-paced, Green screen, Social experiment |
| Clip of the analyzed person | A line from the person being analyzed, up to 4 seconds, that makes sense on its own, followed by an exaggerated label that turns into praise | Clip + "{Name} has {the most X Y} on the internet, in my opinion." | (no collected example yet) | Analysis |

## Patterns that tend to work

1. "{Provocative label} + {surprising result}": debate and curiosity.
2. "{Well-known person} {surprising action}.": the familiar name lowers the effort to get in.
3. "{Strong statement about a conflict everyone lives}": instant debate.
4. "{Sourced data point that scares, about something everyday}": fear, curiosity and familiarity together.

## Notes

- In the method's observation, a hook with concrete data (number, name, fact) holds better than a purely emotional hook. Test it with the creator's audience before treating it as a rule.
- Hooks that open a "who/how/how much" gap > "why".
- Familiarity (a known person or brand) in the hook reduces friction to get in.
- The best hook combines 2 or 3 principles at once.

## How to present the options

Before writing, gather everything you know: the hook method, the video brief's format and structure, the bubble (vocabulary, what moves it, well-liked comments) and what the creator profile says about this person (the hooks that worked and the adjustments they've made to your hooks).

Show 3 to 5 hooks of different types. The number follows the creator's saved preference. With no saved preference, show 3 and ask whether they'd like to see more (up to 5); with their OK, note the answer in the creator profile. Each hook follows the block in [hook](../hook.md) (section 7), with the type and the block where the video pays off on the last line:

```
Hook 1: {the spoken line, 1 to 3 short sentences}
On-screen text: {3 to 5 words, up to 2 lines}
Opening: {what appears in the first 2 to 3 seconds and the movement}
Topic and gain: {what it's about} / {what the viewer gains}
Uses: {principles and addictive elements}
Why it works: {type}, {one line}. The video pays off in {block}.
```

Each option passes the alignment test before it appears: with the sound off, you can tell the topic from the image and the text; listening only, the spoken line matches what's on screen. In the script version, the hook field carries only the spoken line (it's what goes on the teleprompter), and the opening field carries the opening visual and the on-screen text of the chosen hook.

If the video brief doesn't have a recording style yet, show two styles that fit the format alongside, one line each: framing, cutting and on-screen text. Then ask which hook (and which style, if it was missing) the creator prefers, and say two hooks can be mixed.

## The bridge

The bridge is the sentence right after the hook that connects the opening to the rest. It's short: one sentence, two at most. A long bridge burns the seconds in which the viewer decides whether to stay. The next second already delivers value: the tip, the answer or, when the structure saves the answer for the end, a concrete piece of it. It opens the hold zone, the 5 to 10 seconds in which the viewer checks whether the promise holds up and whether the speaker can be trusted ([hold zone](../hold-zone.md)).

## When the creator wants to talk about the hook

- Talk only about the hook. The rest of the script waits.
- Ask, or read in their message, which part they liked. That part stays.
- A hook usually gets lost at the end of the sentence. Offer variations of the ending, keeping the beginning.
- If they say it lacks controversy or curiosity, change what's at stake: who loses, how much, what nobody expected. More adjectives don't fix it.
- With the hook locked, note it in the video brief and only then move on to the script.

## What isn't a hook

- A video comment doesn't become a hook on its own. One about conflict or provocation, with a lot of likes, can be one of the opening options, with the part that grabs kept the same and the topic in the same sentence or the next (see [breaking into a bubble](../breaking-the-bubble.md), section 9). The creator chooses. Outside the opening, it goes in the middle, as the creator's own line (proof, turn, example, reaction).
- A greeting, intro or empty line before the topic ("hey guys", "look what happened to me").
- A long sentence, in the passive voice or with a hard word.
- "I have this problem" when the topic is a problem. Talk to the viewer: "if you're dealing with this".
- A hook that's clear and for the viewer, but with no curiosity at all. Put what most people do next to the faster or unexpected way that works (the four mistakes are in [hook](../hook.md), section 4).
- A rhetorical question instead of a statement.
- "In this video you'll see", "Did you know that", "Stay till the end": they announce instead of showing.
- Invented or exaggerated news with no source.

## How to measure

When possible, record the current hook and the new one with the same body. Compare how much of the audience stays through the first three seconds and the views at 48 hours. Note the result in the creator profile (what worked or what didn't), with their OK. In the belief-shock test, the current hook is A and the shock hook is B; record the result with the numbers.

## Before showing

- **HW1.** Option 1 is a belief shock: the audience's belief flipped inside out, said as an order, confession or absurd consequence.
- **HW2.** The shock is true, or the creator's sincere opinion proven in the video, and the text points to where the video pays off the shock.
- **HW3.** No option promises results ("you'll grow", "you'll make money").
- **HW4.** Each option declares one of the hook types in this piece, and the type matches the video brief's format.
- **HW5.** The options are of different types.
- **HW6.** Each option carries a concrete data point (name, number, fact) or a person or brand the audience knows, when the research has one.
- **HW7.** Each option says in which block of the script the video pays off the hook's question.
- **HW8.** No option opens with a greeting, an announcement ("in this video you'll see", "did you know that", "stay till the end"), a rhetorical question or a video comment without the topic in the same sentence or the next.
